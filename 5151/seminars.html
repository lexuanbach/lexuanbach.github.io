Seminar Program · CO5151

Two seminars, sixteen topics

Every group presents one core topic in Seminar 1 (Weeks 3–6) and one advanced topic in Seminar 2 (Weeks 8–11): a 30-minute talk plus a mandatory 15-minute Q&A/defense. Seminar 1 and Seminar 2 are each 25% of the final grade.

01 · How a seminar session works

Mechanics

Allocation

In Week 1 you rank topic preferences; the instructor allocates one Seminar-1 topic and one Seminar-2 topic per group (published within 3 days), balancing preferences against the presentation rota below. Swaps are allowed only by mutual agreement before Week 2.

The slot: 30′ talk + 15′ Q&A

The time budget is fixed by the syllabus. Talks are cut at 32 minutes — running over is penalized under "structure & delivery," so rehearse against a timer.

Required talk structure

Every talk — Seminar 1 or 2 — must cover, in this order:

  1. Context & problem — why this topic matters, what question the papers answer
  2. Survey of main approaches — the core papers, explained at the mechanism level
  3. Critical analysis — assumptions, evaluation weaknesses, comparisons across methods (not a summary — this is 25% of your grade)
  4. Demo / example — a worked example, trace, or live demo when feasible
  5. Relation to projects & open directions — how it connects to your own project and what remains unsolved

Target 18–22 slides, at least one table comparing methods, and one slide titled "What we don't believe / what the papers don't show."

Submit 48 hours before your slot Slides (PDF), a 1-page annotated bibliography (one critical sentence per core + extension paper), a demo link/video if applicable, and an AI-use statement. Everyone else submits reading notes (≤ ½ page) and 2 critical questions 24 hours before, and fills a peer-critique form in class.

Discussant group

Each talk has a designated discussant group (see the rota) who opens the Q&A with two prepared questions. In the rota below, the group presenting the following week discusses the current talk — so read ahead.

03 · Seminar 1 — Weeks 3–6

Core topics

S1-1

Advanced Reasoning & Planning

ReAct, Reflexion, Tree/Graph-of-Thoughts, LATS, and hierarchical planning — the paradigms that turn a single LLM call into a reasoning process.

Core readings

  • Wei et al. — Chain-of-Thought Prompting (NeurIPS 2022)
  • Yao et al. — ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting (ICLR 2023)
  • Shinn et al. — Reflexion (NeurIPS 2023)
  • Yao et al. — Tree of Thoughts (NeurIPS 2023)

Extension (presenting group)

  • Besta et al. — Graph of Thoughts (AAAI 2024)
  • Zhou et al. — Language Agent Tree Search / LATS (ICML 2024)
  • Kambhampati et al. — LLM-Modulo planning frameworks (ICML 2024)

Must explain in your talk

  • Why intermediate CoT tokens actually help — and why it's an emergent, scale-dependent capability, not a universal fix
  • ReAct's Thought→Action→Observation loop, and what it assumes about action cost and reversibility
  • How Reflexion differs mechanically from ReAct: episodic memory of reflections, and what feedback signal it needs
  • ToT/GoT: what the search actually searches over, and where the evaluator becomes the bottleneck
  • The planning-vs-decomposition distinction — why "the model produced steps" ≠ "the model can plan"
  • A cost/accuracy comparison across at least 3 of these techniques (tokens, latency, or $ per task)

Be ready to defend: What does ReAct assume about observation cost and reversibility?

Where does Tree-of-Thoughts' gain actually come from — search, or the evaluator?

S1-2

Tool Use & MCP

Function calling and the Model Context Protocol at the protocol level — not "how to build an MCP server," but what MCP standardizes and where its security surface is.

Core readings

  • Schick et al. — Toolformer (NeurIPS 2023)
  • Qin et al. — ToolLLM (ICLR 2024)
  • Anthropic — Introducing the Model Context Protocol (2024 launch) + current MCP architecture and spec

Extension (presenting group)

  • Patil et al. — Gorilla (NeurIPS 2024)
  • Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard
  • Google — Agent2Agent Protocol / A2A (2025)

Must explain in your talk

  • The mechanics of native function calling: schema → constrained generation → execution → observation
  • Why tool selection at scale (thousands of APIs) is a distinct problem from calling one tool correctly
  • MCP's host/client/server architecture; tools, resources, and prompts; Elicitation; and current capability discovery
  • What changed since the 2024 launch protocol: stateless data layer, server/discover, and deprecated Sampling
  • What MCP standardizes that ad-hoc function calling did not, and where the M×N → M+N simplification still leaves integration work
  • Why a tool's description and result are untrusted input from the model's perspective
  • A2A vs. MCP: agent-to-agent vs. agent-to-tool, and why they're complementary, not competing

Be ready to defend: Which MCP input creates the largest injection surface, and why?

What does MCP standardize that plain function calling did not?

S1-3

Agent Memory

Short/long-term and episodic/semantic memory architectures — MemGPT's paging model, Generative Agents' retrieval scoring, and what "forgetting" should mean.

Core readings

  • Packer et al. — MemGPT (2023)
  • Park et al. — Generative Agents (UIST 2023)

Extension (presenting group)

  • Sumers et al. — CoALA memory taxonomy (TMLR 2024)
  • Zhong et al. — MemoryBank (AAAI 2024)
  • Liu et al. — Lost in the Middle (TACL 2024)
  • Xu et al. — A-MEM (2025)

Must explain in your talk

  • The distinction between context-window ("short-term") and persistent ("long-term") memory
  • MemGPT's paging/virtual-context mechanism: what triggers eviction, and what the write policy is
  • Generative Agents' retrieval scoring (recency/importance/relevance) and how it was evaluated
  • CoALA's memory taxonomy (working vs. long-term; episodic/semantic/procedural)
  • Why "lost in the middle" means position matters independently of context capacity
  • How memory can be poisoned, and what a write-time or read-time defense would look like

Be ready to defend: What is the write policy in MemGPT — who decides what is remembered?

How can memory be poisoned, and what would you log to detect it?

S1-4

Retrieval-Augmented & Knowledge-grounded Agents

RAG and GraphRAG as tools inside an agent loop — and when retrieval actively hurts rather than helps.

Core readings

  • Lewis et al. — Retrieval-Augmented Generation (NeurIPS 2020)
  • Edge et al. — GraphRAG (2024)

Extension (presenting group)

  • Asai et al. — Self-RAG (ICLR 2024)
  • Jiang et al. — Active Retrieval / FLARE (EMNLP 2023)
  • Gao et al. — RAG survey (2024)

Must explain in your talk

  • The RAG mechanism: retrieve, then condition generation on the retrieved passages
  • Naive RAG's failure on "global" questions, and how GraphRAG's community-summary approach addresses it
  • Self-RAG: what the reflection tokens actually decide (whether to retrieve, and how to critique retrieved content)
  • FLARE: retrieval triggered by the model's own low-confidence tokens, not a fixed schedule
  • At least one concrete case where retrieval measurably hurts accuracy
  • How retrieval fits as one tool inside an agent's decision loop, not a separate pipeline stage

Be ready to defend: When does retrieval reduce accuracy?

How should an agent decide whether to retrieve at all?

S1-5

Coding Agents & Automated SE

SWE-bench and SWE-agent as research objects: agent–computer interface design, evaluation validity, and contamination — not tutorial use of an AI IDE.

Core readings

  • Jimenez et al. — SWE-bench (ICLR 2024)
  • Yang et al. — SWE-agent (NeurIPS 2024)

Extension (presenting group)

  • Wang et al. — OpenHands (ICLR 2025)
  • Xia et al. — Agentless (2024)
  • Xia & Zhang — ChatRepair (ISSTA 2024)

Must explain in your talk

  • SWE-bench's task format (real GitHub issues + hidden tests) and why "Verified" exists (contamination/flakiness filtering)
  • The agent–computer interface in SWE-agent: what custom tools it gives the model, and why that matters for the result
  • Agentless's three-phase (localize/repair/validate) pipeline as a counter-argument to "agents need autonomy"
  • What SWE-bench does not measure (maintainability, security, code-review quality)
  • ChatRepair's conversational repair loop: how test-failure feedback shapes the next patch attempt
  • One concrete ablation or contamination concern for any of these systems

Be ready to defend: What does SWE-bench measure and not measure? Why does "Verified" exist?

Is the agent–computer interface or the underlying model responsible for SWE-agent's gain?

S1-6

Self-improvement & Reflection

Reflexion, Self-Refine, Self-Consistency — and the uncomfortable result that self-correction without an external verifier is weak.

Core readings

  • Madaan et al. — Self-Refine (NeurIPS 2023)
  • Wang et al. — Self-Consistency (ICLR 2023)

Extension (presenting group)

  • Huang et al. — LLMs Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet (ICLR 2024)
  • Lightman et al. — Let's Verify Step by Step (ICLR 2024)
  • Zelikman et al. — STaR (NeurIPS 2022)

Must explain in your talk

  • Self-Refine's loop: the same model generates, critiques, and revises — no external signal
  • Self-Consistency: sampling + majority vote, and why that's a different mechanism from refinement
  • Huang et al.'s finding: why self-correction without external feedback often fails or hurts accuracy
  • The outcome-reward vs. process-reward distinction, and why process supervision helped in "Let's Verify Step by Step"
  • STaR's bootstrap loop: generate rationales, keep the ones reaching correct answers, fine-tune
  • Where each technique's feedback signal actually comes from (self vs. external) — the key critical question for this topic

Be ready to defend: Under what conditions does self-refinement actually work?

What's the difference between an outcome and a process verifier, and why does it matter for search?

S1-7

Agent Design Patterns & Orchestration

Workflows vs. autonomous agents; prompt chaining, routing, orchestrator–worker, evaluator–optimizer — and why the simplest pattern that works is usually the right one.

Core readings

  • Anthropic — Building Effective Agents (2024)

Extension (presenting group)

  • Anthropic — How we built our multi-agent research system (2025)
  • OpenAI — A Practical Guide to Building Agents (2025)
  • LangGraph documentation — state graphs, checkpointing, human-in-the-loop

Must explain in your talk

  • The workflow-vs-agent distinction: fixed code path vs. model-directed control flow
  • At least three concrete patterns (prompt chaining, routing, orchestrator–worker, evaluator–optimizer) and what each buys/costs
  • Why Anthropic argues for the simplest pattern that works, and what triggers moving to a more complex one
  • What "durable execution" / checkpointing means for a long-running agent's failure recovery
  • How the multi-agent research system's lead/subagent split changes cost and latency, not just capability
  • A design-pattern decision your own project actually made, and the alternative you rejected

Be ready to defend: Why does Anthropic recommend against agents for many tasks?

What does an orchestrator–worker pattern buy over one long context?

S1-8

Agent Evaluation & Benchmarking

AgentBench, GAIA, WebArena, τ-bench, and the recurring pitfalls: contamination, judge bias, and cost-blind leaderboards. Directly useful for your project's evaluation.

Core readings

  • Liu et al. — AgentBench (ICLR 2024)
  • Mialon et al. — GAIA (ICLR 2024)
  • Zhou et al. — WebArena (ICLR 2024)

Extension (presenting group)

  • Yao et al. — τ-bench (2024)
  • Kapoor et al. — AI Agents That Matter (2024)
  • Zheng et al. — Judging LLM-as-a-Judge (NeurIPS 2023)

Must explain in your talk

  • What AgentBench/GAIA/WebArena each actually measure, and on what kind of task
  • pass@k vs. pass^k: what each measures, and why pass^k is the stricter, deployment-relevant one
  • τ-bench's reliability finding: why even strong agents fail pass^8 despite a decent pass@1
  • The "AI Agents That Matter" critique: cost-blind leaderboards, inadequate holdouts, conflated benchmarking needs
  • LLM-as-judge's known biases (verbosity, position, self-enhancement) and how to audit for them
  • Contamination as a threat to any benchmark number, and how you'd test for it

Be ready to defend: Why report pass^k instead of pass@k?

What are the failure modes of LLM-as-judge, and how would you audit one?

04 · Seminar 2 — Weeks 8–11

Advanced topics

S2-1

Multi-Agent Systems & Orchestration

AutoGen, MetaGPT, CAMEL — and when a team of agents genuinely beats one agent with better tools.

Core readings

  • Wu et al. — AutoGen (COLM 2024)
  • Hong et al. — MetaGPT (ICLR 2024)
  • Li et al. — CAMEL (NeurIPS 2023)

Extension (presenting group)

  • Qian et al. — ChatDev (ACL 2024)
  • Cemri et al. — Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail? (2025)

Must explain in your talk

  • What AutoGen/MetaGPT/CAMEL actually add structurally (roles, conversation patterns) vs. a single agent with tools
  • At least 2–3 concrete failure categories from Cemri et al.'s taxonomy (system design, inter-agent misalignment, task verification)
  • A fair, cost-matched comparison design: multi-agent vs. single-agent on the same task and budget
  • When a single agent with better tools plausibly wins over a multi-agent team
  • ChatDev's chat-chain structure: how communication phases map onto SE lifecycle stages

Be ready to defend: Is multi-agent buying capability, context isolation, or parallelism?

How do you evaluate a multi-agent system fairly against a single agent at matched cost?

S2-2

Multi-Agent Communication & Collaboration

Debate, role-play, and negotiation protocols between agents — and where emergent behaviour is real vs. an artifact of prompting.

Core readings

  • Park et al. — Generative Agents (UIST 2023)
  • Du et al. — Multiagent Debate (ICML 2024)

Extension (presenting group)

  • Google — A2A protocol (2025)
  • Liang et al. — Divergent thinking via multi-agent debate (EMNLP 2024)

Must explain in your talk

  • Multiagent Debate's mechanism: multiple instances propose and critique each other's answers over rounds
  • Whether debate gains hold up against self-consistency at matched token budget — the core critical question
  • Generative Agents' emergent behavior: what's genuinely emergent vs. an artifact of the simulation's prompting
  • What A2A standardizes for agent-to-agent messaging that MCP does not
  • How agents could collude, and what a detection/monitoring signal would look like

Be ready to defend: At equal token budget, does debate actually beat self-consistency?

How could agents collude, and how would you detect it?

S2-3

Prompt Injection & Attacks

Direct and indirect prompt injection as a system vulnerability, not a model one — the OWASP LLM Top 10 and why prompt-level detectors fail.

Core readings

  • Greshake et al. — Indirect Prompt Injection (AISec 2023)
  • Perez & Ribeiro — Ignore Previous Prompt (2022)
  • OWASP — Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025)

Extension (presenting group)

  • Zou et al. — Universal adversarial attacks / GCG (2023)
  • Liu et al. — Formalizing prompt injection (USENIX Security 2024)
  • Willison — The lethal trifecta for AI agents (2025)

Must explain in your talk

  • The direct/indirect injection distinction, and why indirect is the harder, more dangerous case
  • A concrete indirect-injection scenario for your own project's tools — not a generic example
  • Why "ignore previous instructions"-style detectors are fundamentally unreliable
  • GCG's mechanism at a conceptual level: gradient-guided suffix search, and why it transfers across models
  • The OWASP LLM Top 10 categories relevant to agents specifically, not just chatbots
  • Willison's "lethal trifecta": which of your own project's tools puts it at risk
Responsible red-teaming only Test injection and jailbreak techniques only against your own project's system or sanctioned benchmarks (e.g., AgentDojo). Never test against third-party services.

Be ready to defend: Which of your own project's tools could become an exfiltration channel?

S2-4

Advanced Security & Isolation

Memory poisoning, multi-agent collusion, tool-supply-chain attacks, and defenses by design — sandboxing, least privilege, and information-flow control.

Core readings

  • Chen et al. — AgentPoison (NeurIPS 2024)
  • Debenedetti et al. — AgentDojo (NeurIPS 2024, D&B)

Extension (presenting group)

  • Debenedetti et al. — Defeating Prompt Injections by Design / CaMeL (2025)
  • Beurer-Kellner et al. — Design Patterns for Securing LLM Agents (2025)

Must explain in your talk

  • AgentPoison's mechanism: poisoning memory/RAG stores so a trigger retrieves a malicious demonstration
  • AgentDojo's evaluation design: utility vs. attack-success-rate as two separate axes, not one
  • CaMeL's core idea: separating control flow from data flow so untrusted data can't redirect execution
  • The utility cost of CaMeL's defense, with concrete numbers — security is not free
  • At least 2 of Beurer-Kellner et al.'s design patterns and what each one rules out
  • The minimum permission model you'd apply to your own project's tools

Be ready to defend: What does AgentDojo measure, and what is the utility cost of the best defenses?

What is the minimum permission model for your project's tools?

S2-5

Safety & Alignment

Guardrails, Constitutional AI, human-in/on-the-loop, and action-boundary enforcement — model alignment vs. system safety.

Core readings

  • Bai et al. — Constitutional AI (2022)
  • Ouyang et al. — RLHF (NeurIPS 2022)

Extension (presenting group)

  • Shavit et al. — Practices for Governing Agentic AI Systems (2023)
  • Greenblatt et al. — Alignment Faking in LLMs (2024)
  • Meinke et al. — In-context Scheming (2024)

Must explain in your talk

  • Constitutional AI's mechanism: self-critique against a written constitution, no human harm labels
  • The model-alignment vs. system-safety distinction, with one concrete example of each failing independently
  • Alignment faking: what behavior was observed, and why it complicates "the model says it's aligned" as evidence
  • In-context scheming: at least one concrete strategic behavior models exhibited (disabling oversight, exfiltration)
  • What an "action boundary" or escalation policy would need to look like for your own project

Be ready to defend: Which failures does model alignment address, and which need system-level safety?

What is an "action boundary," and how do you enforce it outside the prompt?

S2-6

Reinforcement Learning for Agents

Verifiable rewards, GRPO, and reasoning-RL (o1/R1-style) — plus reward hacking as the recurring failure mode.

Core readings

  • Sutton & Barto — Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction (ch. 1, 3, 13)
  • Ouyang et al. — RLHF (NeurIPS 2022)
  • Shao et al. — GRPO / DeepSeekMath (2024)

Extension (presenting group)

  • DeepSeek-AI — DeepSeek-R1 (2025)
  • Lightman et al. — Process reward models (ICLR 2024)
  • Pan et al. — SWE-Gym (2024)

Must explain in your talk

  • Why verifiable rewards (not human preference alone) unlocked reasoning-RL at scale
  • GRPO's mechanism relative to PPO: what it removes (the critic) and why that matters for cost
  • DeepSeek-R1's training recipe at a high level: what changes between R1-Zero and R1
  • A concrete reward-hacking example for a tool-using agent — not a generic "reward hacking exists" claim
  • SWE-Gym's role: why a training environment with executable verification, not just more data, was the bottleneck

Be ready to defend: Why did verifiable rewards unlock reasoning-RL?

Give a reward-hacking example for a tool-using agent.

S2-7

Web agents, computer-use agents, and embodied agents — where perception-action grounding still fails on long horizons.

Core readings

  • Wang et al. — Voyager (TMLR 2024)
  • Zhou et al. — WebArena (ICLR 2024)

Extension (presenting group)

  • Xie et al. — OSWorld (NeurIPS 2024)

Individual grading All four criteria are scored per group, except that Q&A performance can move an individual's score within the group: if you cannot defend your own slides, expect a lower personal mark even if your group's talk was strong. There is no separate graded "critique of other groups" line — see the participation bonus below for how asking good questions is rewarded instead.
Participation bonus — asking questions, both rounds Asking a substantive, evidence-based question during Q&A — as an assigned discussant or simply from the floor during any other group's talk — earns participation credit, tracked from live Q&A plus your peer-critique forms. Accumulated across Seminar 1 and Seminar 2, this converts into a bonus of up to +0.5 points added to your final course grade (10-point scale) — on top of the 25/25/50 weighting, not a substitute for it, and capped so your final grade can never exceed 10/10.
06 · Peer critique

What you fill in every session

As a non-presenting student, submit one peer-critique form per talk:

  • One claim in the talk you found well-supported, and why (2–3 sentences)
  • One claim you found unsupported or wrong, with the evidence or paper that contradicts it
  • One question you would ask the authors of the core paper — not the presenters
  • Scores 1–5 on: accuracy · critical depth · delivery · usefulness for your own project

As the discussant group for a talk, prepare two questions in advance and open the Q&A with them. This form is also what the participation bonus above is based on — it is no longer a separately graded rubric line, but asking good questions still pays off.
