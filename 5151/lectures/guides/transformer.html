Paper Guide · Week 1 — Introduction & Methodology

Attention Is All You Need: The Transformer Architecture

The eight-page paper that deleted recurrence and convolution from sequence modeling entirely, replacing both with attention alone — the one architecture underneath every LLM, and therefore every agent, this course studies.

Read the paper ↗ Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez, Kaiser & Polosukhin · NeurIPS 2017 ← Back to Introduction & Methodology
01 · In plain terms

Stop passing notes down a line — let everyone read the whole page at once

Before this paper, the standard way to process a sentence was an RNN or LSTM: read one word, update a small "memory" vector, read the next word, update it again, and so on to the end — like a game of telephone where each person only gets the whisper the previous person passed along. Two problems follow directly from that: information from early words gets diluted by the time you reach the end of a long sentence, and — just as importantly for anyone training these models — you cannot process word 5 until you've finished processing word 4, so the whole computation is stuck being sequential, one step at a time, no matter how many GPUs you own.

The Transformer's idea is almost provocatively simple: let every word look directly at every other word in the sentence, all at once, and let the model itself learn which words matter to which. No passing anything down a chain. No step-by-step bottleneck. Just one large, fully-parallel computation per layer. That single change — replacing recurrence with direct, all-to-all attention — is the entire technical content of the paper's title.

02 · How it works

What came before, and what self-attention actually computes

The state of the art this paper was replacing: Sutskever et al.'s sequence-to-sequence model (2014) used an encoder RNN to compress an entire input sentence into one fixed-length vector, which a decoder RNN then unpacked into the output sentence — a hard bottleneck for anything longer than a short sentence. Bahdanau et al. (2014) fixed the worst of that by adding attention: at each decoding step, instead of relying on one compressed vector, the decoder computes a weighted combination over all of the encoder's hidden states. This was a real breakthrough — but attention was bolted onto an RNN backbone that was still fundamentally sequential. Encoding word 5 still had to wait for word 4. Training on long sequences was still slow, and GPUs — built for massive parallelism — sat mostly idle waiting for each step to finish.

What this paper removes: the recurrence itself. There is no RNN anywhere in the Transformer. Instead:

  • Self-attention — every position computes a Query, a Key, and a Value vector from its own embedding. A position's new representation is a weighted sum of every position's Value vector, where the weight comes from how well that position's Query matches each other position's Key (dot product, scaled, then softmax). This is one matrix multiplication over the whole sequence — not a loop.
  • Multi-head attention — this happens several times in parallel ("heads"), each with its own learned projections, so different heads can specialize (one might track syntactic dependency, another coreference); the results are concatenated and projected back down.
  • Positional encoding — since attention alone has no built-in sense of word order (it treats the sentence as a set, not a sequence), a fixed sinusoidal position vector is added to each word's embedding before anything else happens, so order information survives.
  • Encoder and decoder stacks — the encoder is N identical layers, each self-attention followed by a position-wise feed-forward network (with residual connections and layer normalization around each). The decoder is the same, plus one more sub-layer: masked self-attention (a position cannot attend to positions after it — necessary because the decoder generates output one token at a time at inference) and a cross-attention layer that attends over the encoder's output.

Because every position is processed simultaneously within a layer, training parallelizes completely across sequence length — the single biggest practical reason this architecture won.

The single change the paper's title refers to. Vaswani et al., NeurIPS 2017.
One layer of the encoder–decoder stack (repeated N=6 times in the original paper).
Key result On WMT 2014 English→German, the Transformer reached 28.4 BLEU — a new state of the art among single models at the time; on English→French it reached 41.8 BLEU. The base model trained in roughly 12 hours on 8 GPUs — dramatically less than comparable RNN-based systems, because training parallelized fully across sequence length.
Worked exampleWhat "it" refers to — a self-attention illustration from Google's own explainer

Google Research's companion blog post for this paper (Uszkoreit, 2017) used a now-famous pair of sentences to show what self-attention weights actually capture:

Sentence A"The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired." Self-attentionWhen computing the representation for "it", the model's attention weights concentrate heavily on "animal". Sentence B"The animal didn't cross the street because it was too wide." Self-attentionSame sentence structure, one word changed ("tired" → "wide") — attention for "it" now shifts to "street".

Nothing about the model's weights differs between these two runs — the attention pattern itself is computed fresh from context each time, which is exactly why this mechanism resolves an ambiguity a purely sequential model would have to guess at.

03 · Try it yourself

Train a tiny Transformer, live in your browser

This is a genuinely tiny Transformer — 1 layer, 2 attention heads, ≈720 parameters — that runs and trains entirely in your browser, no server involved. "Training" here means real gradient descent: every number below comes from an actual forward pass through the exact self-attention + feed-forward mechanism described above, and each training step nudges every parameter by a real (numerically-estimated) gradient of the loss. Nothing is scripted or pre-recorded — the model starts from random weights and has to earn its predictions.

It only ever learns two sentences, which is the point: small enough to watch the whole training-then-inference arc in seconds.

04 · For your talk & project

Why every guide after this one assumes you know this

  • Every "LLM" named anywhere in this course — the model inside every agent, every seminar paper, every project — is a Transformer (almost always decoder-only, without the cross-attention block described above). When a paper says "the model attends to earlier tokens," this is the mechanism it means.
  • This is also why Week 3's "context window" and "lost in the middle" are real, specific phenomena and not vague metaphors: attention weights are computed, not uniform, and position affects them — a direct, mechanical consequence of self-attention, not an implementation detail layered on top.
  • For any seminar talk: you can assume your audience already knows what "the Transformer" is at this level of detail — spend your time on what your topic's paper adds on top, not on re-explaining attention from scratch.
  • A sharp question this raises for your own project: does your chosen model's context window actually behave the way you assume across its full length, or have you tested that?

06 · Since Transformer

A lineage, not a single leap

The original paper described one architecture used one way: an encoder reading a full source sequence, a decoder generating a target sequence, wired together by cross-attention — built for machine translation.
