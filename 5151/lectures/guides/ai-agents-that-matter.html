Paper Guide · Week 1 — Introduction & Methodology

AI Agents That Matter

The methodology critique behind every rubric line in this course that says “accuracy alone is not evaluation” — required reading before you defend any benchmark number, your own or someone else's.

Read the paper ↗ Kapoor, Stroebl et al. · arXiv 2024 / TMLR 2025 ← Back to Introduction & Methodology
01 · In plain terms

A leaderboard number without a cost number is half an answer

Agent benchmarks report one number: accuracy on some task set. Kapoor et al.'s argument is that this is systematically misleading, because it's silent on cost. An agent that spends $50 of API calls retrying, self-critiquing, and voting to gain one extra accuracy point over a $2 baseline isn't "better" in any useful sense — it's a different point on a cost/accuracy trade-off that the leaderboard hides. The paper's practical demonstration: they took an existing HotpotQA agent (built with the DSPy framework) and re-optimized it for cost, cutting spend substantially while preserving accuracy — proof that most published agent numbers are not on the efficient frontier of what's actually possible.

02 · How it works

Pareto frontiers, not leaderboards

The paper's fix is to plot every agent as a point in (cost, accuracy) space and ask which points are Pareto-optimal — no other point beats them on both axes at once. When Kapoor et al. did this across published agent designs (including Reflexion and LATS, both already in your reading list), the result was uncomfortable: several sophisticated, widely-cited agents sit behind the frontier traced by much simpler baselines. Their added complexity (extra reasoning steps, self-critique passes, tree search) costs real money and barely moves accuracy — the opposite of what the original papers' single-number tables suggested.

The paper also raises three more concrete methodology failures worth naming individually: benchmarks that don't control for contamination (the test set leaked into training data), holdout sets too small to distinguish real improvement from noise, and evaluation protocols that conflate "can this agent do the task at all" with "can this agent do the task reliably, every time" — the same distinction Week 3's pass^k metric (τ-bench) is built to expose.

Worked exampleApplying the critique to your own project

Suppose your project's evaluation table reports: "our agent: 78% accuracy; baseline: 71% accuracy." Kapoor et al.'s checklist forces three more columns before that comparison means anything:

Cost$/task for each configuration — if your agent costs 6× more per task, a 7-point gain may not be a real win. Seeds/varianceIs 78% the mean of ≥3 runs, or one lucky run? Report variance, not a single number. ContaminationCould any test task's answer have appeared in the model's training data or your few-shot examples?

This is exactly the shape of R5 (Quantitative evaluation) in your project's grading gate — the rubric's "≥2 configurations, ≥3 seeds, cost and latency" language is this paper's methodology, operationalized.

04 · For your talk & project

What this buys you

  • If you present S1-8 Agent Evaluation & Benchmarking: this paper is the critical spine of the whole topic — every other benchmark (AgentBench, GAIA, WebArena) should be evaluated through this paper's lens (does it report cost? variance? contamination controls?), not next to it as an unrelated fourth reading.
  • For your project's D1 proposal and D3 report (R5, R9): cite this paper by name when you justify why your evaluation reports cost alongside accuracy — it is the methodological authority the rubric's evaluation criterion is built on.
  • A sharp critical question for any seminar talk citing a benchmark leaderboard: "what would this ranking look like if cost were held fixed across all agents?"
