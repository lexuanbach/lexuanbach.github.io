Foundations: Agent Reasoning & Planning

Chain-of-Thought, ReAct, Reflexion, Tree/Graph-of-Thoughts, and the planning debate — what each technique's mechanism actually is, what it assumes, where its cost goes, and how to tell a genuine structural gain from a compute-budget artifact.

Learning objectives

By the end of this session, you can

  1. Explain the mechanism — not just the name — of Chain-of-Thought, self-consistency, ReAct, Reflexion, Tree-of-Thoughts, and Graph-of-Thoughts, and reproduce a worked trace for each.
  2. Place each technique on a common cost/accuracy trade-off axis and state the assumption each one makes about the availability of a reward or evaluation signal.
  3. Distinguish "reasoning" from "planning" in the classical AI sense, and explain the core objection to the claim that LLMs can plan on their own.
  4. Choose, for a given project task, which reasoning/planning paradigm is appropriate and justify the choice against at least one alternative.
01 · Chain-of-Thought and its variants

Why intermediate tokens help

Wei et al. (NeurIPS 2022) showed that prompting a sufficiently large language model to produce intermediate reasoning steps before its final answer — rather than answering directly — substantially improves accuracy on multi-step arithmetic, symbolic, and commonsense reasoning tasks. The mechanism is simple to state and easy to underestimate: the model's own previously-generated tokens become part of its context for generating the next token, so writing out "step 1, step 2, step 3" effectively gives the model a scratchpad, decomposing a hard single-shot prediction into several easier ones conditioned on each other.

GSM8K math word problems, PaLM 540B, 8-shot prompting. Wei et al., NeurIPS 2022.
Direct prompting:
  Q: Roger has 5 tennis balls. He buys 2 cans of 3 balls each. How many does he have now?
  A: 11                                    ← often wrong for weaker models, no visible working

Chain-of-Thought prompting:
  Q: Roger has 5 tennis balls. He buys 2 cans of 3 balls each. How many does he have now?
  A: Roger started with 5 balls. 2 cans of 3 balls each is 2 × 3 = 6 balls.
     5 + 6 = 11. The answer is 11.

Two nuances worth holding onto for the whole semester:

  • CoT is a capability that must already exist, elicited, not created. The original paper shows the effect emerges only at sufficient model scale (an "emergent ability") — CoT prompting a small model with weak reasoning does not help and can even hurt. This is why "just add 'let's think step by step'" is not a universal fix — it surfaces a capability, it does not install one.
  • Explanations are not guaranteed to be faithful. There is no guarantee the stated "steps" reflect the actual computation — treat CoT output as an artifact that helps the model, not as a transparent log of its reasoning. This matters directly for Reflexion and process-reward methods later in this note.

Zero-shot CoT and self-consistency

Zero-shot CoT (Kojima et al., 2022) showed that simply appending "Let's think step by step" to a prompt — with no worked examples at all — recovers much of the benefit of the original few-shot CoT prompting. This separates two contributions that are easy to conflate: (a) reasoning stepwise helps, and (b) few-shot exemplars help — zero-shot CoT shows (a) alone accounts for a large share of the gain.

Self-consistency (Wang et al., ICLR 2023) treats CoT as a stochastic process: sample multiple independent reasoning chains (via temperature > 0), then take a majority vote over their final answers, discarding the individual chains.

def self_consistency(question, model, n_samples=10):
    answers = []
    for _ in range(n_samples):
        chain = model.generate(cot_prompt(question), temperature=0.7)
        answers.append(extract_final_answer(chain))
    return majority_vote(answers)

The insight: individual chains can go wrong in different, largely uncorrelated ways, so voting recovers a signal that a single deterministic chain would miss. The cost is explicit: `n_samples` times the tokens and latency of a single chain, for a typically sub-linear accuracy gain — your first concrete example of the test-time compute trade-off that recurs all semester (ToT, LATS, and reasoning-RL models like o1/DeepSeek-R1 are all, at some level, ways of spending more inference-time compute for a better answer).

02 · ReAct — synergizing reasoning and acting

Reasoning grounded in real observations

CoT reasons about a static context; it cannot acquire new information mid-reasoning. ReAct (Yao et al., ICLR 2023) interleaves free-text reasoning ("thoughts") with concrete actions against an external environment, feeding each action's observation back into context before the next thought.

ReAct's core loop. Yao et al., ICLR 2023.
Thought 1: I need to find out who directed the film that won Best Picture in 2023.
Action 1: Search["2023 Best Picture Oscar winner"]
Observation 1: "Everything Everywhere All at Once" won Best Picture at the 95th Academy Awards.
Thought 2: Now I need to find the director(s) of that film.
Action 2: Search["Everything Everywhere All at Once director"]
Observation 2: Directed by Daniel Kwan and Daniel Scheinert.
Thought 3: I have the answer.
Action 3: Finish["Daniel Kwan and Daniel Scheinert"]

This is precisely the control loop from Lecture 1's anatomy section, with the reasoning step split into an explicit Thought (why) and Action (what) pair. The thought is not executed by anything — its only function is to condition the action on an explicit intermediate justification, which the paper shows reduces hallucinated or off-task actions compared to acting without a reasoning step, and reduces reasoning drift compared to CoT without grounding in real observations.

What ReAct assumes

  • Actions are cheap and fast. A search API call is cheap; a paid transaction or a multi-minute simulation is not — the paper's benchmarks all have cheap, fast, reversible actions. Lecture 3's discussion of tool irreversibility is the direct sequel to this gap.
  • The action space is small and well-specified. A handful of named actions with clear semantics. Real agents (covered in Week 2 of this course) often expose dozens to hundreds of tools — the tool-selection problem barely exists in ReAct's original setting.
  • Observations are immediately truthful and complete. No adversarial content in tool results — an assumption Seminar 2's security topics (S2-3, S2-4) directly attack: if an observation can contain attacker-controlled text, "feed the observation back into context" is exactly the mechanism indirect prompt injection exploits.
Where does the gain come from? A healthily skeptical question for any reasoning-augmentation paper: is the improvement coming from the technique's structure, or simply from spending more tokens? ReAct's own ablations show acting-only and reasoning-only both underperform the combination — a genuine structural claim, not just a compute-budget artifact. Demand this same ablation for every technique in this course.
03 · Reflexion — verbal reinforcement learning

Learning across attempts without updating weights

Reflexion (Shinn et al., NeurIPS 2023) adds a second phase after a ReAct-style attempt fails: the agent reflects in natural language on why it failed, and stores that reflection in an episodic memory buffer that is included in context on the next attempt at the same or a similar task.

Reflexion's multi-episode loop — no weights change, only what's in the prompt. Shinn et al., NeurIPS 2023.
def reflexion(task, model, evaluator, max_trials=4):
    memory = []                                    # episodic memory of past reflections
    for trial in range(max_trials):
        trajectory = react_loop(task, model, context_extra=memory)
        outcome = evaluator(trajectory)             # success/failure signal -- see below
        if outcome.success:
            return trajectory
        reflection = model.generate(reflect_prompt(trajectory, outcome))
        memory.append(reflection)                   # persists to the next trial
    return trajectory  # last attempt, possibly still failing

The name "verbal reinforcement learning" is precise in one sense and misleading in another: like RL, the agent improves across attempts at the same task using a feedback signal — but no weights are updated; the "learning" is entirely the accumulation of natural-language reflections in a growing context. This is why it belongs here rather than in S2-6 (RL for Agents), which covers actual gradient-based training.

The critical dependency: where does the evaluator come from?

Reflexion's mechanism is only as good as its feedback signal, and this is the single most important thing to interrogate in the paper and in any system that claims "self-improvement." Three cases, in decreasing order of trust:

  1. External, ground-truth evaluator (e.g., unit tests pass/fail). Strong, reliable signal — Reflexion's coding-task results fall here.
  2. External, heuristic evaluator (e.g., an LLM judge scoring the trajectory). Weaker — inherits whatever biases the judge model has.
  3. The same model reflecting on its own output with no external signal at all. Shown to be unreliable by Huang et al. (ICLR 2024): without external ground truth, models cannot reliably tell correct answers from incorrect ones well enough to usefully revise them, and self-correction can even decrease accuracy by talking the model out of a correct answer.
Rule of thumb for this course Whenever a paper or your own project claims "the agent reflects and improves," ask immediately which case above the feedback signal falls into. Case 3 dressed up as case 1 is the most common inflated claim you will encounter — directly relevant to your project's R6 security analysis and R5 evaluation requirements: an agent that "self-verifies" its own security posture with no external check has verified nothing.
04 · Tree-of-Thoughts and Graph-of-Thoughts

Reasoning as search

CoT and ReAct both commit to a single linear sequence of steps — once a thought is generated, the model doesn't reconsider it. Tree-of-Thoughts (Yao et al., NeurIPS 2023) instead treats reasoning as a search problem over a tree of partial solutions, with four components: a thought generator, a state evaluator, a search algorithm (BFS/DFS in the original paper), and a stopping rule.

Tree-of-Thoughts search: two branches pruned, one kept

Graph-of-Thoughts (Besta et al., AAAI 2024) generalizes further: thoughts form an arbitrary graph, allowing operations like merging two partial solutions or refining a thought using another thought as feedback — useful when a good solution is best assembled from combining several partial, individually-imperfect thoughts.

Where does ToT's gain actually come from?

The single most important critical question for this topic: is the improvement coming from exploring more of the solution space, or from the quality of the state evaluator that decides what to prune? ToT's evaluator is also an LLM call — so it is only as reliable as the same underlying model being asked a different question ("is this partial solution promising?"). ToT's real cost-benefit trade-off: spend O(branches × depth) extra LLM calls in exchange for a search structure that can recover from a bad first guess — but the ceiling on how well it can recover is set by how good the model already is at judging partial solutions, which is not obviously easier than solving the problem directly.

LATS (Language Agent Tree Search, Zhou et al., ICML 2024) combines this tree search with actual environment interaction — Monte Carlo Tree Search over ReAct-style trajectories, using real observations rather than only LLM-judged simulation — the "what if the evaluator were partly external/grounded" answer to the concern above.

05 · Planning: task decomposition, hierarchical planning, and world models

Why "planning" needs a precise meaning

In classical AI, planning has a precise meaning: given a formal model of the world (states, actions, preconditions, effects), find a sequence of actions that provably transforms an initial state into a goal state, often with guarantees. This is a much stronger claim than "the model produced a plausible-looking numbered list of steps." When a paper says an LLM "plans," ask whether it means the classical sense (provable, verifiable) or the colloquial sense (generated a sequential decomposition that is not verified against anything).

Useful patterns, roughly in order of how much of the "planning" is delegated to a verifiable external system versus the LLM alone:

LLM+P hands verification to a classical planner; world models are the least mature pattern here.
  • Task decomposition — the LLM breaks a goal into an ordered list of sub-tasks in natural language, with no formal verification. The weakest and most common form.
  • Plan-and-execute (Wang et al., ACL 2023) — explicitly separate a planning LLM call (produce the full sub-task list up front) from execution LLM calls, rather than interleaving them as ReAct does.
  • Hierarchical planning — decompose recursively: a high-level plan's steps are themselves sub-goals that get their own sub-plans, bottoming out at directly executable actions.
  • LLM+P (LLM-to-Planner) — use the LLM only to translate a natural-language task into a formal PDDL problem description, then hand the actual search off to a classical, sound-and-complete planner.
  • World models — learn or use an explicit predictive model of how the environment's state changes in response to actions, and search or reason over that model's predicted future states. Least mature of these patterns for language agents.

The central objection: "LLMs can't plan, but can help planning"

Kambhampati et al. (ICML 2024, "LLM-Modulo" frameworks) make an empirically-grounded argument that LLMs, used alone, are unreliable at classical planning — they cannot verify their own plans against formal correctness criteria, and their apparent "planning" performance degrades sharply on harder instances or adversarial rephrasing. Their proposed LLM-Modulo framework keeps the LLM in an idea-generation role while routing verification and refinement through external, sound critics.

The position to defend or challenge "Planning" claims about LLMs split cleanly into (a) claims that are actually about task decomposition — real and useful, but with no correctness guarantee — and (b) claims that are actually about classical planning, which current evidence suggests LLMs alone do not do reliably. When you encounter "planning" in a paper, or design a "planning module" for your project, state explicitly which of these two you mean.
06 · In-class demo

Same task, three paradigms

Run the same small task (e.g., a Game-of-24-style puzzle, or a short multi-hop question) under three conditions, with token and wall-clock cost logged for each: (1) plain CoT, single sample; (2) ReAct with access to a calculator/search tool; (3) Tree-of-Thoughts with a small branching factor. Discuss: which condition got the right answer? At what token/latency cost? 07 · Discussion questions

For this session

  1. For a task in your own project idea, which row of the cost/accuracy table fits best, and what would make you switch to a different row after seeing real results?
  2. Reflexion's feedback signal — for your project, what will the evaluator actually be, and which of the three cases (external ground-truth / external heuristic / no external signal) does it fall into? If it's case 3, what would you need to add to move it to case 1 or 2?
  3. Kambhampati's objection targets "planning" specifically — does the same objection apply to Reflexion's "reflection," or to ToT's "evaluation"? What would distinguish a reliable self-assessment from an unreliable one in each case?
  4. Self-consistency spends n× compute for a vote. Tree-of-Thoughts spends branches × depth compute for a search. Under what condition would you expect self-consistency to beat ToT despite being structurally simpler?
Takeaway

If you remember nothing else from today

Every technique in this lecture trades inference-time compute for accuracy along the same axis — more tokens, more branches, more self-critique generally buys a better answer, until it doesn't. The question that separates a real result from a compute-budget artifact is always the same one: where does the gain actually come from, and is it worth the cost at the scale your own project runs at? Ablate before you believe a technique — including the ones you choose for your own architecture.
